Automating Computational Hydraulics: A Comprehensive Architectural Specification for Parsing SWMM5 Input Files to Synthesize Nano Banana Prompts1. Introduction: The Convergence of Deterministic Modeling and Generative AIThe discipline of urban water management stands at a precipice of technological transformation. For over five decades, the United States Environmental Protection Agency’s Storm Water Management Model (SWMM) has served as the global gold standard for simulating the rainfall-runoff process and the hydrodynamics of conveyance systems.1 It is a deterministic engine, grounded in the rigorous conservation of mass and momentum, operating on a dataset defined by explicit physical parameters. However, the emergence of advanced multimodal artificial intelligence—specifically the class of models referred to in recent literature as "Nano Banana" or Gemini 2.5 Flash Image 2—offers a new paradigm: the capability to interpret, visualize, and reason about these complex systems through semantic prompting rather than raw numerical execution.This report serves as an exhaustive technical specification for developing an application capable of bridging these two worlds. The objective is to parse the legacy ASCII structure of the SWMM5 input file (.inp), extract a high-dimensional feature vector based on element counts and topological density, and synthesize this data into a "Nano Banana Prompt." This prompt is not merely a summary; it is a structured cognitive instruction set designed to enable a generative AI to reconstruct the "mental model" of the drainage system, assessing its complexity, identifying dual-drainage coupling, and visualizing its spatial extent.To achieve this, the application must navigate the historical strata of the .inp format, ranging from its Fortran-based origins to the modern additions of SWMM 5.2.4 It must distinguish between purely hydrologic objects and hydraulic controls, recognize the nuances of third-party software exports 6, and handle the erratic syntax of user-generated comments. This document provides the theoretical and algorithmic foundation for such a system, encompassing nearly 15,000 words of detailed analysis, parsing logic, and prompt engineering strategy.1.1. The SWMM5 Input Architecture: A Historical and Structural OverviewThe SWMM5 input file is a deceptive artifact. On the surface, it appears to be a simple flat-text database. However, its structure is a palimpsest of fifty years of software evolution, retaining vestiges of the original 1971 punched-card formats while accommodating modern object-oriented parameters.1 The transition from SWMM4, which relied on rigid 80-column spacing, to SWMM5’s free-format tokenization marked a significant shift in usability but introduced new complexities for automated parsing.1The file is divided into sections, delimited by bracketed keywords (e.g., , ). These sections are not required to appear in any specific order, although the EPA’s graphical user interface (GUI) typically writes them in a logical sequence beginning with and.7 A robust parser cannot rely on this sequence; it must act as a state machine, transitioning its parsing logic based on the detection of these headers.Furthermore, the introduction of SWMM 5.2 has radically altered the schema for hydraulic links, introducing the and sections to support dual-drainage modeling—simulating flow both inside the pipe and on the street surface simultaneously.5 Parsing these new sections is non-negotiable for a modern application; failing to detect them would misclassify a complex urban flooding model as a simple pipe network.1.2.         Clean Line: Trim whitespace

        Else:
            # Default Element Counting
            counts[current_section] += 1

Return counts, metadata
